Cervical cancer screening and treatment, close to point of care.

UnaVix is developing a complete pathway for cervical cancer screening, on-site triage and treatment of precancer, for communities far from a laboratory. We are looking for partners and funders to pilot it.

Partner with us Our approach

In 2022, cervical cancer took the lives of 348,189 women1.

Almost 94% of those deaths were in low- and middle-income countries1. It is one of the most preventable cancers2, but many women there cannot reach the services that would prevent it.

Three gaps stand between these women and care:

Results take weeks

Samples travel to a central laboratory. Many women cannot make the long journey back for their results and are lost before treatment.

Too few pathologists

Reading cytology needs trained cytotechnologists and pathologists, who are scarce and concentrated in city hospitals.

Manual staining varies

Pap staining by hand depends on timing and handling. Inconsistent slides are harder to read and less reliable.

Sources

  1. Bray F. et al., Global cancer statistics 2022: GLOBOCAN estimates of incidence and mortality worldwide for 36 cancers in 185 countries, CA Cancer J Clin, 2024.
  2. World Health Organization, Global strategy to accelerate the elimination of cervical cancer as a public health problem, 2020.

One complete pathway, on site

We follow the WHO's screen, triage and treat approach. Our goal: a woman goes from her first test to treatment without travelling to a city laboratory.

1 · Screen

HPV self-test

Each woman takes her own sample in private, and it is tested for high-risk HPV on site.

2 · Triage

Cytology, read remotely

A cervical sample is taken from HPV-positive women and prepared on a slide. Our automated Pap stainer stains it on site, and a pathologist reads it through our telecytology portal.

3 · Treat

Thermal ablation

Women with precancerous lesions are treated on site. Suspected cancers are referred to a hospital.

Our roadmap

  1. 1
    Pilot 1 · Planned in Cameroon

    Testing in the field

    Women take an HPV self-sample in their community. We compare two approaches: the sample is sent to an existing laboratory for testing, or it is tested on site and women get their result the same day. HPV-positive women are then triaged using our slide preparation and telecytology tools.

    Key questions

    • Does on-site HPV testing work in remote communities?
    • How quickly can a woman receive her triage result under real-world conditions?
    • What proportion of HPV-positive women successfully complete the recommended pathway, from screening to triage and treatment?
  2. 2
    Pilot 2 · Based on the results of Pilot 1

    Same-day full pathway

    Women take an HPV self-sample and are tested on site. HPV-positive women are triaged by telecytology and, if needed, receive treatment in the same visit. This would be a prototype of our Loop clinic.

    Key questions

    • What proportion of HPV-positive women successfully complete the pathway with same-day screening, triage and treatment?
    • Do women’s attitudes towards treatment change when it is offered in the same visit, rather than by referral?
    • How can a community sensitisation programme increase turnout and acceptance of same-day treatment?
    • What training do local staff need to deliver the full pathway safely and consistently?
    • What logistics are needed to run a Loop clinic?
  3. 3
    Scale-up · Based on the results of Pilot 2

    Scaling up Loop

    Rolling out Loop within national and NGO screening programmes, with UnaVix providing the equipment, telecytology portal and training.

    Key questions

    • How many women can each Loop clinic screen and treat per year?
    • Do completion rates stay high when Loop is run by national and NGO programmes?
    • How quickly can a new site be set up and its local team trained?
  4. 4
    Pilot 3

    Two-day screening and treatment

    Women are tested for HPV on day one, and HPV-positive women come back on day two for triage and treatment. This lets each team screen many more women per day, at the cost of a second visit. It would be a prototype of our Wave clinic.

    Key questions

    • Does a two-day approach increase loss to follow-up?
    • How does the cost per woman treated compare with same-day care?
