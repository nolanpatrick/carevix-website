The technology behind CareVix

Every step of our pathway uses a method that works outside a central laboratory. Here is what each one is, and why we chose it.

HPV self-sampling HPV testing Cytology triage Why cytology Thermal ablation

Screen

HPV self-sampling

What it is

After a short demonstration by a nurse, each woman takes a vaginal swab herself, in private. No speculum exam is needed for screening.

Why we use it

Many women are more willing to be screened when they can take the sample themselves. The WHO recommends self-sampling as an option for HPV screening, and it lets a small team screen many women in a day.

Screen

HPV testing

High-risk HPV causes about 99% of cervical cancers, and the WHO recommends HPV testing as the primary screening method. We use two platforms, one for each solution.

QIAGEN careHPV, for Wave

Detects the DNA of high-risk HPV types. Samples are tested together in one run of up to 90, which takes about three and a half hours. Built for high-volume screening in low-resource settings.

Cepheid GeneXpert, for Loop

A cartridge-based test that runs each sample individually, with a result in about an hour. That's what makes same-day screening and treatment possible.

Triage

Cytology triage

Why triage at all

Most HPV infections clear on their own. A positive test shows a woman is at risk, not that she has a lesion. Triage finds the HPV-positive women who actually need treatment, so the others aren't treated unnecessarily.

How it works

A nurse collects cells from the cervix. They are placed on a slide, Pap-stained on site by GyroStainer, scanned, and read by a pathologist through our telecytology platform.

Triage

Why cytology, and not VIA or histology

Cytology looks at the cells themselves, like histology, but needs no biopsy. With GyroStainer and telecytology it can be done where the woman is, with a result the same day or the next.

Treat

Thermal ablation

What it is

A portable device heats a metal probe to about 100 °C. The probe is applied to the abnormal area of the cervix for 20 to 40 seconds at a time, which destroys the precancerous cells. The tissue then heals on its own over the following weeks.

Between women, the probe is cleaned and disinfected by boiling.

Why we use it

The WHO recommends thermal ablation to treat cervical precancer. The device is small and battery-powered, the procedure takes minutes and usually needs no anaesthesia, and trained nurses or doctors can perform it. That means women can be treated on site, in the same programme that screened them.

It suits lesions that are fully visible and not too large. Other lesions, and any suspicion of cancer, are referred to a hospital.
